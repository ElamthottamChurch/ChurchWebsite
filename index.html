About Our Church

The church's origins trace back to the early 19th century when the Latin Rite Carmelites, based in Varappuzha, administered over the Syriac Christians. In 1872, with the support of local Christian families and under the guidance of Melus Methra, a church was founded in Elamthottam in honor of St. Antony the Abbot. In 1882, Chaldean Metropolitan Thondanat Mar Abdisho, consecrated by Melus Metropolitan, established this church as his episcopal seat and commenced his ecclesiastical administration. Following his passing in 1900, the church and its properties were entrusted to the Roman Catholic Church. In 1911, under Bishop Mar Kuryalassery of the Changanassery Diocese, the church became a sub-parish of Pala Valiya Church. Later, on December 21, 1966, Bishop Mar Sebastian Vayalil elevated it to the status of a parish church. ​

St. Anthony the Abbot

Anthony the Great (c. 251–356), also known as Anthony of Egypt or Anthony the Abbot, was a Christian monk from Egypt who is venerated as the Father of Christian Monasticism. Renowned for his ascetic lifestyle, he gave up his wealth to live in the desert, seeking spiritual purity and solitude. His life and teachings, chronicled by Athanasius of Alexandria, inspired the monastic tradition across both Eastern and Western Christianity. Anthony is also remembered for resisting numerous temptations, including visions of demons, and is invoked as a patron against skin diseases like "St. Anthony's Fire." His feast day is celebrated on January 17.

Early Life and Background: Anthony the Great was born around 251 AD in the village of Koma, near Herakleopolis in Egypt. He came from a wealthy Christian family and was raised in the faith. After the death of his parents, Anthony inherited their fortune and responsibility for his younger sister. Inspired by a sermon in which Jesus said to “sell all you have and give to the poor,” Anthony gave away his inheritance and began a life of spiritual devotion.

Path to Asceticism: Anthony first lived near his village, adopting a simple and disciplined lifestyle under the guidance of local hermits. He later withdrew into the desert, seeking greater solitude to deepen his relationship with God. There, he practiced extreme fasting, prayer, and meditation. He stayed in an abandoned fort for over 20 years, where he reportedly encountered and resisted demonic temptations, earning him a reputation for spiritual strength.

Spiritual Struggles and Teachings: Anthony’s life was marked by intense spiritual battles. He often spoke of facing demons and temptations in physical and mental forms. These struggles became a powerful symbol in Christian literature and art. His teachings emphasized humility, obedience, and the power of prayer. He encouraged others to live a pure and disciplined life and warned against pride and materialism.

Influence on Monasticism: Though he lived as a hermit, Anthony attracted followers who were inspired by his way of life. These disciples eventually formed communities based on his teachings. While he did not organize monasteries himself, he is still considered the "Father of Christian Monasticism" because of his lasting impact. His spiritual biography, The Life of Anthony, written by Saint Athanasius, played a significant role in spreading monastic ideals throughout both the Eastern and Western Christian worlds.

Later Life and Legacy: In his later years, Anthony moved to Mount Colzim near the Red Sea, continuing his solitary life but occasionally offering advice to visitors. He died at the age of 105 in 356 AD. After his death, his teachings and example lived on, inspiring countless monks and ascetics across centuries. He is venerated as a saint in many Christian traditions and is regarded as a patron against skin diseases, especially ergotism, which was known as "St. Anthony’s Fire." His feast day is celebrated on January 17.
